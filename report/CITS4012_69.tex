\title{Knowing Where to Look Is Not Knowing What Is Right:\\
A Difference-Aware Transformer for Physical Commonsense QA}

\author{Group 69}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
PIQA asks which of two near-identical solutions achieves an everyday physical goal. We ask whether a model trained from scratch, without external data, can learn \emph{where} the two solutions differ, and whether that is enough. Our Difference-Aware Contrastive Transformer (\dact{}) makes the difference explicit, with difference-tag embeddings, cross-solution attention and difference-guided pooling, and adds a jointly trained lexical head. The answer is yes to the first question and no to the second. Attention controls show that \dact{} learns to put 86\% of its pooling attention on the differing words (uniform attention would give 25\%), and ablations confirm that marking the difference helps. But knowing where to look is not knowing what is right: \dact{} is the best from-scratch model on validation (61.2\%, 3 seeds), yet on test it is statistically tied with a TF-IDF baseline (61.6 vs.\ 61.2\%, McNemar $p{=}0.88$), and pretrained models are 5--14 points better. Wrong predictions attend to the differing words even more than correct ones: the bottleneck is physical knowledge, not focus. We also report a pre-registered negative result and the failures that shaped the design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Physical commonsense, knowing that \emph{baby wipes} rather than \emph{index cards} remove chalk dust, is easy for people and hard for machines. PIQA \citep{bisk2020piqa} tests it with a goal and two candidate solutions, exactly one of which is physically sensible. The task matters for assistants that give practical instructions and for robots that must choose feasible actions. Its key difficulty is that the two solutions are usually near-duplicates: in our training split the median share of differing solution tokens is 15\%, so the answer hinges on a few substituted words whose plausibility requires world knowledge.

\paragraph{Related work.} On PIQA, pretrained models are far from people: \citet{bisk2020piqa} report 77.1\% for RoBERTa-large against 94.9\% for humans, and multi-format QA training improves pretrained models further \citep{khashabi2020unifiedqa}. Our setting differs, because the main model must learn from scratch. The closest architectural ideas come from multiple-choice reading comprehension and inference. The Option Comparison Network \citep{ran2019option} and DCMN+ \citep{zhang2020dcmn} compare candidate answers with each other instead of scoring them independently, and ESIM \citep{chen2017esim} and the decomposable attention model \citep{parikh2016decomposable} align two texts token by token and build comparison features. \dact{} adapts these ideas to two near-identical solutions: difference tags and cross-solution attention make the alignment explicit, and difference-guided pooling focuses the decision on the substituted words. Finally, work on annotation artefacts shows that shallow lexical cues can solve many items without reasoning \citep{gururangan2018annotation,poliak2018hypothesis}; our TF-IDF baseline and lexical head make this effect measurable on PIQA.

Our aim was a model that is trained from scratch but built around this structure. \dact{} (Section~\ref{sec:method}) makes the difference between the solutions explicit at every stage: in the input, in the comparison and in the summary vector, and it adds a lexical head that scores which words make a solution plausible. We compare it with five baselines, from majority vote to a zero-shot LLM, run eight ablations with three seeds each, and analyse the attention with controls and probes.

Our main findings are: (i) a from-scratch model does learn where the options differ: the attention focus on the differing words is learned, not built in, and removing the difference tags lowers accuracy; (ii) this is not enough: \dact{} is the best from-scratch model on validation but statistically tied with a strong lexical baseline on test; (iii) errors come from \emph{judging} the differing words without physical knowledge, which only pretraining supplies. Section~\ref{sec:history} documents what worked and what did not during development.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{figures/architecture.pdf}
\caption{\dact{}. Both options pass through the same weights, so for already-encoded inputs swapping the options swaps the scores (verified in the notebook). The full pipeline is not exactly equivariant, because the \texttt{difflib} alignment depends on option order (Section~\ref{sec:history}).}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Input and difference tags.} For a goal $g$ and solutions $s_1, s_2$, each option $k$ is encoded as $x^{(k)} = [\textsc{cls}]\,g\,[\textsc{sep}]\,s_k\,[\textsc{sep}]$ with a BPE vocabulary \citep{sennrich2016bpe} learned on the training split. We align the token sequences of $s_1$ and $s_2$ with \texttt{difflib} and give every solution token a tag $t_i \in \{\textit{shared}, \textit{different}\}$ (goal and special tokens: \textit{other}). The input embedding is
\begin{equation}
e_i = E_{tok}[x_i] + E_{pos}[i] + E_{seg}[\sigma_i] + E_{tag}[t_i].
\end{equation}
The tag embedding (our contribution) tells the encoder from the first layer where the options disagree.

\paragraph{Encoder.} A shared pre-LayerNorm Transformer encoder \citep{vaswani2017attention,xiong2020layer} maps each option to hidden states $H^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$.

\paragraph{Contrastive cross-solution attention.} Each token of option $k$ attends to the solution tokens of the rival option $\bar{k}$ plus its \textsc{cls} token as a ``no counterpart'' sink:
\begin{equation}
c_i = \mathrm{MHA}\big(h^{(k)}_i, H^{(\bar{k})}, H^{(\bar{k})}\big),
\end{equation}
and the states are updated with the comparison features of ESIM \citep{chen2017esim}:
$\tilde{h}_i = \mathrm{LN}\big(h_i + \mathrm{FFN}([h_i; c_i; h_i - c_i; h_i \odot c_i])\big)$.
Adapting ESIM's local inference from premise--hypothesis pairs to two competing answers is our design choice: plausibility is relative, so each option is represented by what it has \emph{instead of} its rival.

\paragraph{Difference-guided pooling.} Over the solution tokens we use additive attention \citep{bahdanau2015neural} with a learned bias per tag (our extension):
\begin{equation}
\alpha_i \propto \exp\big(v^\top \tanh(W \tilde{h}_i) + b_{t_i}\big),\quad p = \textstyle\sum_i \alpha_i \tilde{h}_i,
\end{equation}
with $b_{\textit{different}}$ initialised to 1 and the others to 0. A scorer MLP maps $[p; \tilde{h}_{\textsc{cls}}]$ to a scalar $s_k$.

\paragraph{Lexical head.} Following the wide \& deep idea \citep{cheng2016wide}, a sparse linear component scores the words of each solution directly: every BPE unigram and bigram of $s_k$ is hashed into one of $2^{18}$ buckets with learned weight $w_j$ (initialised to 0),
\begin{equation}
\ell_k = \textstyle\sum_{j \in \mathrm{ngrams}(s_k)} w_{h(j)},\qquad z_k = s_k + \ell_k.
\end{equation}
It is trained jointly with the Transformer, with its own learning rate and weight decay. Under the pairwise softmax only $\ell_1 - \ell_2$ matters, so shared n-grams cancel and the head learns which substituted words indicate a plausible solution, the signal that a TF-IDF baseline exploits.

\paragraph{Objective.} The two scores compete in a softmax, $P(k) = \mathrm{softmax}(z_1, z_2)_k$, trained with cross-entropy and label smoothing 0.1, which matches the forced-choice task. Every component can be switched off in a single configuration object, which is how the ablations are run.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\paragraph{Data.} PIQA provides 16{,}113 training items and 1{,}838 labelled development items; the latter serve as our test set. Test labels are used only in the final evaluation step (enforced by a guard in the notebook), although the test file is loaded and tokenised at the start and B1/B3 compute (but do not inspect) their test predictions earlier. An audit of the data found six duplicate rows within the training file and one test item (index 1545) that appears twice in our training split. We keep the split unchanged and disclose the overlap: re-scoring the saved test predictions without that item changes accuracy by about 0.02 points (\dact{} 61.57\,$\to$\,61.55\%, TF-IDF 61.21\,$\to$\,61.19\%). We hold out a stratified 10\% of the training file (seed 42) for validation: 14{,}501 / 1{,}612 / 1{,}838 items, all label-balanced (50.0 / 50.0 / 50.5\% label 1). Text is whitespace-normalised, NFKC-normalised and lower-cased. Goals are truncated to 40 and solutions to 96 BPE tokens; for longer solutions (1.6\% of test solutions) we keep the 96-token window centred on the differing span, not the prefix (Section~\ref{sec:history}).

\paragraph{Training.} AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} ($\beta_2{=}0.98$, weight decay 0.05, learning rate $3{\times}10^{-4}$; lexical head $10^{-2}$), 6\% linear warm-up then cosine decay, batch 128, gradient clipping 1.0, dropout 0.2, at most 20 epochs with early stopping on validation accuracy (patience 6), bf16 mixed precision on a Colab A100. Step 1 of the notebook selects the configuration on validation with one seed from five candidates: base ($d{=}256$, 4 layers) 61.0\%, \textbf{small ($d{=}128$, 2 layers, 4 heads) 61.3\%}, base with dropout 0.3 60.9\%, and base/small with a 10-epoch in-domain masked-LM warm-up \citep{devlin2019bert} 60.9 / 61.0\%. The selected model has 1.93\,M parameters, 262\,k of them in the lexical head. The lexical head's learning rate was chosen in a separate validation pilot (Section~\ref{sec:history}).

\paragraph{Baselines.} All baseline numbers come from our own runs. \textbf{B0} majority class. \textbf{B1} TF-IDF of 1--2-grams of $s_2 - s_1$ with logistic regression, $C$ tuned on validation; it tests how far shallow lexical cues go \citep{gururangan2018annotation}. \textbf{B2} a 2-layer BiLSTM with additive attention and the same tokenizer (5.1\,M parameters), a recurrent model of similar budget. \textbf{B3} \texttt{roberta-base} \citep{liu2019roberta} fine-tuned with a multiple-choice head (4 epochs, learning rate $2{\times}10^{-5}$, 3 seeds, best epoch on validation), representing pretrained encoders. \textbf{B4} \texttt{Qwen2.5-1.5B} \citep{qwen2025qwen25} zero-shot: the prompt ``Goal: $g$ / Solution:'' followed by each solution, choosing the higher length-normalised log-likelihood, which measures LLM knowledge without any training.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Accuracy is PIQA's official metric; labels are balanced and each item has exactly one correct option, so it is unbiased with 50\% chance level. Trained models are run with seeds 42--44 and reported as mean $\pm$ std. For the seed with the best \emph{validation} accuracy we give a 95\% bootstrap CI (2{,}000 resamples; \citealp{efron1993bootstrap}) and an exact McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947note} against \dact{} on the test set.

\paragraph{Validity of the test numbers.} Validation accuracy is the only number that reflects design decisions. The test set was evaluated in several development runs, and the lexical head was introduced after earlier test results had been seen (Section~\ref{sec:history}). Test numbers are therefore reported for completeness and comparison, not as a clean held-out estimate of generalisation.

\paragraph{Overall comparison.} \dact{} is the best from-scratch model on both splits (Table~\ref{tab:main}): +1.7 / +1.5 points over the BiLSTM (not significant on test, McNemar $p{=}0.10$) and +2.0 / +0.4 over TF-IDF. On test, \dact{} and TF-IDF are statistically tied (61.6 vs.\ 61.2\%, McNemar $p{=}0.88$): \dact{}'s advantage over the lexical baseline is on validation, and the error analysis below shows where each model wins. We do not claim that \dact{} beats it. Pretraining dominates: RoBERTa is 5.1 and the zero-shot LLM 13.8 points above \dact{} on test, in line with PIQA being designed to test knowledge rather than reading skill \citep{bisk2020piqa}.

\paragraph{Ablations.} Each ablation tests one hypothesis about a design choice (Table~\ref{tab:abl}); everything else, including seeds, data and budget, is fixed. \emph{Marking and comparing the difference helps}: removing the tags costs 1.3 / 1.0 points and removing cross-solution attention 0.7 / 0.9. \emph{Direct lexical evidence helps}, mainly on validation: removing the lexical head costs 1.8 / 0.4. The forced-choice objective helps on validation only (independent binary scoring: 0.7 / 0.0). Removing everything costs 2.5 / 2.4 points, the largest drop. Attention pooling makes little difference (0.1 / 0.6), and the tag bias, including its initial value, makes no consistent difference (removing it gives +0.5 / +0.2): these components overlap with the tags, which already tell the model where the options differ. Differences below one seed std are not conclusive, and with seven comparisons single p-values should not be over-read \citep{dror2018hitchhiker}.

\paragraph{Complementary errors.} \dact{} and TF-IDF agree on 77.9\% of test items; \dact{} alone solves 11.2\% (205 items) and TF-IDF alone 10.9\%, for an oracle accuracy of 72.4\%. The items only \dact{} solves typically have a one-word difference inside long shared text (\emph{water} vs.\ \emph{soda}; the typo \emph{sparrow bottle} vs.\ \emph{spray bottle}), where a bag-of-words difference vector has almost nothing to work with. Against RoBERTa the oracle rises to 83.6\%.

\label{fig:controls}

\paragraph{Is the attention focus learned?} The trained model puts 85.9\% of its pooling attention on differing tokens, which make up 24.8\% of solution tokens (Figure~\ref{fig:controls}). Because the tag bias starts at 1, we separate the sources: an untrained model reaches only 38.9\%, while the trained model with the bias set to 0 still reaches 75.1\%, and the bias itself barely moved in training (1.0\,$\to$\,1.019). The focus is therefore learned by the pooling scorer, which also explains why initialising the bias at 0 changes nothing. Attention weights are not a complete explanation of a decision \citep{jain2019attention,wiegreffe2019attention}, so we use them as evidence of where the model looks, not of why it decides.

\paragraph{Why does the neural part alone not beat bag-of-words?} We tested two explanations on validation. \emph{H1: the pooling does not select} is rejected: the normalised pooling entropy is 0.49 (1 = uniform), for correct and wrong predictions alike ($p{=}0.18$). \emph{H2: the difference signal is missing}: a linear probe separating differing from shared tokens reaches 59.0\% balanced accuracy on the vanilla Transformer's embeddings and only 59.9\% after its encoder, so without tags the encoder barely works out where the options differ, whereas in \dact{} the tag remains perfectly decodable (100\%) in every layer. Tags and the lexical head thus supply information the encoder does not learn from 14.5\,k items. Crucially, wrong predictions put \emph{more} attention on the differing tokens than correct ones (87.7 vs.\ 84.7\%, $p{<}0.001$): looking at the right words is not the bottleneck; judging them is.

\paragraph{Qualitative analysis.} For the item \emph{adding scents to lotion bars} (Figure~\ref{fig:cases}, top), the wrong option adds the oils ``to the water in the bottom of the double boiler''. The pooling peaks on \emph{bottom} (of the double boiler), the part that makes the option wrong, yet the model prefers it with p = 0.99: it locates the decisive span but cannot know that oil in the water bath never reaches the lotion. In \emph{to safely sleep with your baby} (bottom), pooling selects the differing spans on both sides, but the scores tie: the choice needs infant-safety knowledge. Successes rest on the same word-level evidence: for \emph{a simple camping lantern}, the gold option (a milk jug on top of an \emph{LED light}, \emph{translucent plastic}) wins with p = 1.00 against \emph{fairy lights} in a filled jug, with pooling spread over the many differing words. In an earlier run, \emph{baby wipes} vs.\ \emph{index cards} for chalk dust was solved confidently; the training split contains \emph{baby wipe(s)} only in the correct option 26 times and only in the wrong option 5 times, so the confidence comes from word statistics, not from reasoning about absorbency. Finally, the most confident error, \emph{boil water for a cup of tea} (p = 0.99), shows the lexical head's calibration problem: the wrong option adds a whole extra sentence, and since $\ell_k$ sums over n-grams, long differing spans receive extreme scores.

\paragraph{What worked.} Difference-aware truncation removed inputs that had become identical after prefix truncation (in run 1, one item differed only at BPE positions 97--98). The attention controls (Figure~\ref{fig:controls}) turned the worry that the focus was built in by the bias initialisation into a test, which it passed. Three RoBERTa seeds exposed fine-tuning instability: in run 2 one seed stayed at chance (53.7\% validation), a known failure \citep{dodge2020finetuning}. Giving the lexical head its own learning rate made it useful.

\paragraph{What did not.} The first lexical head barely moved from zero before early stopping. The goal-matching block (165\,k parameters) was pre-registered, with its specification, acceptance rule (validation gain $\geq$ 1.0 point and $>$ 2 pooled seed std) and decision procedure committed before training (\texttt{experiments/goal\_matching/}); it lost 0.3 points on validation and was rejected without a test run. On the free T4, emulated bf16 slowed training from 108 to 377\,ms per step until bf16 was restricted to GPUs that support it natively. Between run 3 and the final run, single ablations and baselines moved by up to 1.4 points, so one-point differences are noise.

\paragraph{Protocol, audit and evidence.} The lexical head was configured on validation only, but the decision to explore it was taken after the test results of runs 1 and 2 had been seen, so the small test gap to TF-IDF is indicative, not a clean held-out result. An independent audit of run 1 (\texttt{docs/PROJECT\_AUDIT.md}) found the training/test overlap (Section~3), the order-dependent alignment (103 test items in the original pipeline, 102 now; an opt-in canonical alignment removes it), early loading of the test file and unsaved checkpoints, and added prediction export, provenance and validation checks. The submitted notebook contains the complete final run (commit \texttt{0d70cdc}; 57 cells, no errors, logs of all 30 runs) and a cell that checks tables against the result files; \texttt{docs/DEVELOPMENT\_LOG.md} records every step, including the failures.

\section{Conclusion}
\dact{} shows that task-specific structure pays off even when training from scratch: making the difference between two near-identical solutions explicit and adding direct lexical evidence yield the best from-scratch model on validation, although on test it only ties a lexical baseline; controlled ablations support the difference tags and cross-solution attention on both splits and the lexical head mainly on validation, while attention pooling and the tag bias add little. Its attention demonstrably learns to focus on the decisive words. The analysis equally shows the limit of this approach: the model finds \emph{where} the options differ but judges \emph{which} is right with word statistics from 14.5\,k items, leaving it 5--14 points behind pretrained models.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Improvements of about one point are within seed and test-sample noise; hyper-parameters were selected with one seed; the lexical head is over-confident on long differing spans; the test comparison with TF-IDF is not fully clean (Section~\ref{sec:history}); one test item overlaps with the training data; the difference tags depend on option order (102 test items change tags when the options are swapped; the opt-in canonical alignment removes this); the model overfits quickly, with training accuracy at 89--92\% against about 61\% on validation at the selected epoch (97--98\% by the last epoch), so early stopping does most of the regularisation; and ablations that remove modules also change parameter counts. An explicit goal-matching block did not help in a pre-registered validation test (Section~\ref{sec:history}); natural next steps are length-normalised lexical scores and combining the difference-aware design with pretrained representations.
